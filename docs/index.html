San Francisco’s heat map is drawn by fog, not by income.

In most American cities, surface heat tracks poverty. Here it doesn’t — the marine layer overrules everything, and it doesn’t care who lives underneath it. But the shade that people build is another story.

What the satellite can’t see

The west is measured only on its warmest days

Known bias, quantified

A thermal sensor cannot see through fog, so the foggiest neighborhoods are observed almost exclusively on their rare clear days — which are their warm days. This is not a bug to be fixed; it is a hard limit of the instrument. What you can do is measure it.

Ranked by heat vulnerability

Moderate confidence

The index inherits every uncertainty above — clear-sky bias in its exposure half, survey margins in its sensitivity and capacity halves. Ranks near each other should be read as ties, not as an order. Ranks 20 places apart are real.

The index combines exposure (heat, vegetation, fog), sensitivity (residents over 65 and under 5, poverty) and a capacity gap (income, renting, limited-English households). Each variable is percentile-ranked, averaged within its component, and the three components combined by geometric mean — so strength in one cannot fully cancel exposure in another.

Race and ethnicity are deliberately not inputs. Had they been, the finding that vulnerability falls hardest on communities of color would be circular. Kept out, it is a result: the index correlates — with the share of residents of color.

Method & limits

What this is, and what it isn’t

Surface temperature is not air temperature. Landsat measures the skin temperature of roofs and pavement, which runs far hotter than the air a person stands in. The spatial pattern is meaningful; the absolute values are not comparable to a weather station.

Daytime only. Landsat passes late morning. Night-time heat retention is the more health-relevant variable and is not measured here.

Three neighborhoods are parks — Golden Gate Park, McLaren Park and Lincoln Park — with between 58 and 160 residents. Their census rates are noise, so they are mapped but never scored or ranked.

Neighborhood median income is an approximation. Medians cannot be summed, so it is a household-weighted mean of tract medians. Every other rate is exact — summed numerator over summed denominator, which is what population weighting means.

41 units is coarse. Real inequity exists within neighborhoods and is invisible at this resolution.
