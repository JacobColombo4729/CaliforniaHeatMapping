How the heat vulnerability index is calculated

index = ( exposure · sensitivity · capacity_gap )1/3

exposure100 · mean[ ρ(heat_anomaly), ρ(−ndvi), ρ(−fog) ]
sensitivity100 · mean[ ρ(pct_65_plus), ρ(pct_under_5), ρ(pct_poverty) ]
capacity_gap100 · mean[ ρ(−median_income), ρ(pct_renter), ρ(pct_limited_english) ]

Each of the three is published as its own column, so any neighborhood’s index can be recomputed from the table below the Explore section.

ρ(x) is the percentile rank of x among the n = 38 scored neighborhoods, computed as rank(x, method='average') / n, so ρ ∈ (0, 1] — never 0, which would annihilate the product. Negation orients a variable so that higher ρ always means more vulnerable.

Where it’s hot

Every neighborhood, five ways

Heat is an anomaly — degrees above or below the citywide average that day — so a hot afternoon can’t masquerade as a hot neighborhood.

Hatching marks neighborhoods where the satellite has fewest usable observations, on the two layers built from cloud-free days. Fog and income aren’t affected.

heat_anomaly °C — how it is computed 
per scene   LST°C   = ST_B10 · 0.00341802 + 149.0 − 273.15
            anomaly = LST°C − mean(LST°C over cloud-free SF land, same scene)
per pixel   median(anomaly) across scenes where that pixel was cloud-free
            requires ≥ 5 such scenes, otherwise no value
per nhood   mean over the neighborhood's pixels

Source Landsat Collection 2 Level-2, asset lwir11 (band ST_B10). 63 summer scenes, 2019–2024.

Normalising each scene to its own citywide mean is what stops a hot day reading as a hot neighborhood. The result is degrees relative to the city that day, not an absolute temperature.

Heat against income

Neighborhood heat anomaly by median household income

One dot per scored neighborhood; the line is a least-squares fit. Click a dot to open that neighborhood.

Analysis

The relationship is flat at —, and demographics alone — income and poverty together — explain 4% of heat variance (R² = 0.040 at tract level, n = 232). Physical geography explains roughly twenty times as much.

That flat line is two effects cancelling, not the absence of one. A mediation model with a spatial block bootstrap separates them: income buys canopy and canopy cools (indirect −0.032 °C per $10,000, CI [−0.055, −0.005]), while income also raises heat directly (+0.041 °C, CI [+0.007, +0.072]) because the wealthiest ground here is also the densest and most built. Net effect indistinguishable from zero.

Treat the 38-neighborhood figure as underpowered on its own — it can only resolve |r| ≥ 0.32. The tract-level replication is what carries this.

The two fields are near mirror images — heat against fog —, heat against canopy —. Fog is the single largest term in every model fitted here, and adding it lifts R² from 0.41 to 0.77.

Fog is distributed by geography, not by wealth — its correlation with income is only —. That is why the heat–poverty pattern documented in most US cities is flattened here: the dominant driver is handed out indifferently.

fog share 0–1 — how it is computed 
per obs     cloud = QA_PIXEL bit 1 | 2 | 3 | 4
                    (dilated cloud, cirrus, cloud, cloud shadow)
            valid = not fill
per pixel   fog = Σ cloud / Σ valid
per nhood   mean over the neighborhood's pixels

Source Landsat Collection 2 Level-2, asset qa_pixel.

Built from every observation, not only the clear ones — a scene useless for temperature is a data point for fog.

Analysis

Canopy cools almost as strongly as fog, but unlike fog it is unevenly distributed: greener neighborhoods are richer (—), and at tract level vegetation correlates −0.36 with the poverty rate. This is the part of the exposure picture that is built rather than given, and therefore the part a city can act on.

Cooling bought per 0.1 NDVI

Against heat vulnerability

Analysis

Letting the effect vary is decisively better — AICc improves by 233, R² 0.753 → 0.938, and 200 of 232 tracts show a significant local canopy effect at the corrected alpha. Trees buy roughly 1.7 °C per 0.1 NDVI in the Outer Richmond and Inner Sunset, and close to nothing in South of Market, Bayview or the Financial District.

Local cooling power correlates +0.66 with fog — trees work best where the marine layer is already thickest, the opposite of the obvious prediction. Mechanism is not established: fog drip sustaining transpiration is plausible and documented in this climate, but range restriction also contributes (cooling correlates +0.43 with within-neighborhood NDVI spread).

Operationally this cuts against the intuitive intervention. Cooling bought and vulnerability run opposite at —: planting delivers least where the need is greatest. Not an argument against street trees in the Mission — an argument that canopy alone will not close that gap, and that albedo and impervious-surface measures likely dominate in the dense east.

ndvi −1 to 1 — how it is computed 
per scene   reflectance = DN · 0.0000275 − 0.2
            ndvi = (nir08 − red) / (nir08 + red), clipped to [−1, 1]
per pixel   median(ndvi) across cloud-free scenes
per nhood   mean over the neighborhood's pixels

Source Landsat Collection 2 Level-2, assets nir08 and red.

Stands in for tree canopy. The DataSF canopy layer is 289,219 polygons, and street-tree points would miss every park and back yard.

What the satellite can’t see

The west is measured only on its warmest days

A thermal sensor can’t see through fog, so the foggiest neighborhoods are observed almost only on their rare clear — and therefore warm — days. A hard limit of the instrument, not a bug. Usable scenes run from — to — out of 63 — and since a scene is only usable when it isn’t cloudy, that count is the fog map seen from the other side. The foggiest places are the least-measured places, necessarily.

Moderately measured

Nothing is excluded for being foggy. The filter is binary and per-observation, taken from Landsat’s own quality band: a single pixel-reading is dropped if it is flagged cloud, cloud shadow, cirrus or dilated cloud — and those discards are exactly what the fog layer counts. The only quantitative floors are that a scene must cover at least half the window (62 of 125 failed), a pixel needs at least 5 clear looks before it gets a temperature, and pixels reading as water in more than half their clear looks are masked out.

At neighborhood level nothing is filtered at all. The tiers above are confidence labels, not gates. Lakeshore’s 14 scenes at 74% fog are in every statistic on this page — flagged as least certain, never removed.

Analysis

Every temperature on this page understates how much cooler the west really is. The foggiest neighborhoods are sampled almost entirely on their clear — and therefore warm — days, so the true fog effect exceeds the — measured, and the canopy inequity is a floor rather than a ceiling.

This is a sampling limit of thermal satellite imagery, not something a better model fixes. Closing it needs an all-weather ground source — dense air-temperature sensors would do it, and would address the surface-versus-air gap at the same time.

Method & limits

What this is, and what it isn’t

Blocks marked “Analysis” are generated interpretation, not measurement. Every figure quoted inside one is computed by the pipeline and reproducible from data/; the reading placed on those figures is commentary and should be treated as such. Charts and tables carry only measured values.

This is a static snapshot. Current conditions and forecast feeds are not connected — the status strip at the top says which sources are live. The page reads a single artifact, docs/data.js, so a live feed replaces that file rather than rewiring any chart.

Surface temperature is not air temperature, and Landsat passes late morning — so nothing here describes the night, which matters more for health. The spatial pattern is meaningful; the absolute values aren’t comparable to a weather station.

Three neighborhoods are parks — Golden Gate Park, McLaren Park, Lincoln Park, 58 to 160 residents. Mapped, never scored. Median income is approximate (medians can’t be summed, so it’s a household-weighted mean of tract medians); every other rate is exact. 41 units is coarse — inequity within neighborhoods is invisible here.

Two things remain unresolved. Residual spatial clustering was never explained: after fog, canopy, terrain and built-up surface, Moran’s I on the residuals is still +0.433 at p < 0.0001. And the mediation result reverses if the built-up index joins the controls — both specifications are in Explore.

What was dropped, and on whose authority

Every filter in the pipeline, what it removed, and where the number came from. Most are judgement calls of mine — only the census reliability bands come from an outside authority, and the cloud flags come from the sensor’s own product definition rather than from me.

RuleRemovedWhere the number comes fromWhat would change it
Cloudy observations discarded — QA bits for cloud, cloud shadow, cirrus and dilated cloud varies by pixel USGS — the Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 QA_PIXEL definition, produced by their CFMask algorithm. Not my classification. A less conservative mask — keeping cirrus, say — would retain more scenes but admit contaminated pixels.
Scene must cover ≥ 50% of the window 62 of 125 scenes My choice. Each scene is normalised to its own citywide mean, and a sliver of the city gives a mean that isn’t citywide. Normalising against a fixed baseline instead of a per-scene mean would remove the need for this rule entirely.
Pixel needs ≥ 5 clear looks to get a temperature 110,673 px, mostly water My choice, with no external basis. A floor is necessary; five is not a special number. A longer record would support a higher bar. Raising it trades coverage for stability, and the foggy west pays that cost first.
Water masked where a pixel reads water in > 50% of clear looks 41.7% of the grid My choice — a majority rule on the QA water flag. Little. Bay and ocean are unambiguous; only the shoreline pixel or two is genuinely borderline.
Neighborhoods under 500 residents are not scored 3 parks; also 1 tract, the Farallones My choice, but the data makes it robust: the next-smallest neighborhood has 3,901 residents, a 24× jump, so anything between 200 and 2,000 selects exactly the same three. Nothing short of a threshold above 3,901, which would start removing real neighborhoods.
Confidence tiers at 20 and 30 usable scenes nothing — labels only My choice. Purely descriptive. Moving them relabels neighborhoods; it changes no number anywhere on this page.
Census estimates flagged unreliable above a 0.30 coefficient of variation, cautionary above 0.15 nothing — flags only US Census Bureau published reliability guidance. The only threshold here with an outside authority behind it. Nothing, short of the Bureau revising its own guidance.
